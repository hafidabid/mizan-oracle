Mizan Oracle · how it works

A Sharia board decides. Mizan keeps the record and shares its status.

Each member of a Sharia board signs their own opinion on a DeFi product. Mizan checks every signature, counts the opinions, and stores who said what on the blockchain. Apps then read the result and apply their own rules.

Part 1 · smart contract flow

From signed opinions to an on-chain record

Follow one assessment. In this example, three mock board members review a product: two say Approved, one says Needs review. The board needs at least 2 matching opinions (the quorum).

8 steps
  1. Each board member signs their own opinion built M1 and M3 sign Approved. M2 signs Needs review. A different opinion is still signed and kept.

    Each signature is a typed EIP-712 message, ShariaBoardMemberOpinion(Issue issue, uint8 opinion). The Issue covers the product address, version, final outcome, evidence file hash and link, dates, the previous record ID and a one-time number (nonce).

    Change any field later and the signature stops matching.

  2. The assessment API serves the signed package planned The signed opinions and the public evidence file are made available from a fixed address.

    Not built yet. The API can deliver signatures but cannot change them.

  3. The CRE workflow builds the report planned Chainlink CRE fetches the package, checks the chain for the current record, and encodes a version 2 report.

    Report = (2, action 0, Issue, Opinion[]). Opinions are sorted by member address so nobody can be counted twice.

    Not built yet. This is the next task for the CRE bounty.

  4. The forwarder delivers it on-chain mock locally The Chainlink forwarder calls onReport(metadata, report) on the Mizan receiver.

    In the local demo a MockForwarder stands in. It checks nothing and is for Anvil only.

  5. The receiver checks how it arrived built It only accepts the trusted forwarder and the expected workflow.
    • Caller is the configured forwarder
    • Workflow ID and workflow owner match
    • Report version is 2 and action is issue (0) or revoke (1)
    • Then it calls the registry. It makes no other calls.
  6. The registry checks who signed built Every opinion must come from an active board member with a valid signature. At least 2 members must sign.
    • Caller is the receiver, and the board is not suspended
    • Product has code, the dates make sense, the evidence link is 1–512 bytes
    • previousId equals the current latest record (no stale update)
    • Delivery deadline not passed, nonce never used
    • Members in ascending address order, each active, each signature valid
    • 2 members or more signed ✓
    • 2 opinions match the proposed “Approved”, so it takes effect ✓ (if not, the record is still stored, as “Needs review”)
  7. The registry stores the record and the opinions built Record #1 keeps each member's opinion and the count: 2 approved, 0 rejected, 1 needs review, out of a board of 3.

    getOpinions(1) → (M1, Approved), (M2, NeedsReview), (M3, Approved). The latest pointer for the product moves to #1. Older records stay readable forever.

  8. Apps read the facts and decide built resolve(product) returns the outcome, whether it has expired or been revoked, whether the board is active, and the count of opinions.

    Each app chooses its own rule. See part 2.

Try it: what does the registry store?

Change each member's opinion and the proposed outcome. This uses the same rule as the registry (quorum 2 of 3). The counts and names are public whenever at least 2 members sign, even if they disagree.

Part 3 · business-wide

Who does what, from a product to a user's decision

Mizan sits between the people who review a product and the people and apps that rely on that review. The board keeps authority. Mizan makes the result easy to find, check and use.

6 steps
  1. A product team asks for a Sharia review They share the contract address, version, documents and how the product earns returns.

    The review covers one product version. Approving a token does not approve every pool or strategy that uses it.

  2. The board reviews under a named policy Each member gives their own opinion: approved, rejected or needs review. An AI assistant may draft summaries, but cannot approve or sign. AI planned

    The public evidence file states the scope, the exclusions and the reasons. Its hash is part of every signature.

  3. Mizan records the signed opinions The record is stored on the blockchain with each member's opinion, even when the board is split. The outcome only takes effect if enough members agree (the quorum).

    Delivery runs through Chainlink CRE (planned). Nobody in between can change the result without breaking the signatures.

  4. People see a readable record Who reviewed it, how many agreed, what was covered, why, when it was issued and when it expires.

    An old screenshot of an approval cannot hide an expiry or a revocation. The explorer shows the live status.

  5. Apps apply their own rules A wallet, swap or vault can require “current approval” before a deposit. Another app can show a warning only.

    Mizan reports facts. The app decides. A Uniswap hook is one optional example, not the product.

  6. Reviews renew and history stays When a review expires or the product changes, the board reviews again. It can revoke a record with a reason. Old records are never deleted.

    An expired approval stays “approved, expired”. It does not turn into “rejected”. A revoked record never brings an older approval back.

Most hackathon oracles move prices. Ours moves an accountable human decision, and keeps the disagreement visible.

BUIDL page text (copy and edit)

One-liner
Mizan Oracle: Sharia reviews that DeFi apps can check, with every board member's opinion on-chain, delivered by Chainlink CRE.
Short description
Many people only use financial products that follow Sharia rules. In DeFi, they cannot easily see who reviewed a product, what the review covered, or whether it is still valid. Mizan is an oracle for Sharia reviews. Each member of a Sharia board signs their own opinion. A Chainlink CRE workflow fetches the signed opinions from an external API and writes them on-chain. Our registry checks every signature, stores who said what, and needs a quorum before an outcome takes effect. A split board is stored as “Needs review”, so disagreement stays visible. Expiry and revocation are tracked without deleting history. Apps read the facts and choose their own rule. This demo uses a mock board. It is not a real Sharia certification.

Questions judges may ask

Isn't this just a multisig?

A multisig only says yes. Mizan stores each member's own opinion, the counts, the policy and evidence hash, expiry and revocation. Apps can read all of that on-chain.

Who are the scholars?

Today it is a mock board. We never invent names or credentials. The next step is a pilot with one qualified Sharia board.

Why use a blockchain?

Apps can check the review at the moment of a transaction, and nobody can quietly edit or delete a past decision.

Why Chainlink CRE?

The reviews live off-chain. CRE moves them on-chain through a known workflow. The receiver checks the workflow, and the registry still checks every member's signature.

What if one member's key is stolen?

One key alone cannot publish or revoke anything; at least the quorum must sign. The owner can deactivate that member.

How do you make money?

Our hypothesis: subscriptions for review teams and API access for apps. Fees never depend on a positive result. We have not validated this with customers yet.

What is real today
  • Registry, receiver and example consumer contracts are built, with 64 passing Foundry tests and an Anvil smoke test.
  • The board in this page is a mock board. No real product is certified, and no real person is shown.
  • The assessment API, the CRE workflow and the AI assistant are planned, not built. There is no public deployment yet.
